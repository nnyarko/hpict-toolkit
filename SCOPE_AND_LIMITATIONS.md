# HPI-CT: Scope, Intended Use & Limitations

*Read this before using the toolkit for any decision. Personal-capacity, public-source work. Version 1.1.0, 2026-11-11.*

## What this is

HPI-CT is an **educational self-assessment aid** for healthcare fraud, waste, and abuse (FWA) prevention. It helps an organization structure a self-review of its FWA controls against widely used internal-control and compliance frameworks, and see where its own maturity may be strong or thin. It is a **thinking tool and a gap-analysis scaffold**: a structured way to ask better questions and organize evidence.

## What this is NOT

- **Not legal advice.** Nothing here is legal, regulatory, actuarial, or professional advice. Regulatory citations are provided for orientation and must be read in their current, official form (eCFR) and applied with qualified counsel.
- **Not an official standard or government product.** It is not issued, endorsed, reviewed, or approved by CMS, HHS-OIG, DOJ, GAO, COSO, the ACFE, or any regulator or standard-setter. References to those frameworks are for mapping and education only.
- **Not a certification, audit, or guarantee of compliance.** A high maturity score does **not** establish that an organization is compliant, nor does a low score establish a violation. The tool cannot detect fraud and does not opine on any specific transaction, claim, or party.
- **Not a replacement for an organization's compliance program.** It is meant to **complement** an OIG-based compliance program, the COSO/ACFE Fraud Risk Management Guide, and any GRC tooling, not substitute for them.

## Methodology limitations (read these honestly)

- **Scores reflect expert-judgment design choices, not empirically validated measurement.** The hidden scoring keys, the 0–3 maturity scale, the component weights (CE 20 / RA 20 / CA 25 / I&C 15 / Mon 20), the evidence-reliability ladder, and the provenance rules are reasoned design decisions grounded in the cited frameworks. They have **not** been validated through a pilot, and no inter-rater reliability or predictive-validity study has been conducted. There is, as yet, **no evidence that a given maturity score predicts actual FWA exposure or recovery outcomes.**
- **Self-assessment carries self-report bias.** Answers are self-reported; the evidence-reliability ladder and the provenance discriminator mitigate but do not remove this. The "verification status" and provenance fields record *how* verifiable a basis is; they do not independently verify it.
- **Targets and seeded values are illustrative defaults, not benchmarks.** Replace them with your own risk-based targets.
- **Framework mapping is editorial.** Which COSO/Green Book principle, FRMG principle, or OIG element a given question "best" satisfies is a judgment call open to reasonable disagreement. (Framework numbering was checked against the primary sources, and the Green Book numbering again against the 2025 revision in November 2026. Regulation citations were checked in October 2026 by AI readers: of 313 citation items on the first pass, 132 were fully supported as the control states them, 158 partly supported, 10 wrong and 13 could not be verified. The 10 rated wrong were corrected, as were one registry row rated wrong and seven other control citations rated partly supported (17 controls and one registry row changed in all). A further pass on 11 November 2026 corrected the partly supported citations whose correction was clear from the regulation's current text (among them CD-09, PE-03, FR-02, FR-11, CD-08 and REG-001); 152 partly supported items were left as they are, each with a recorded reason, so a citation may still be broader or narrower than the control states. Citations are now labelled, but no person outside the project has re-checked them. Read each citation at its source.)
- **Regulations change.** Citations are current as of the version date above; verify against eCFR before relying on any of them.
- **Inclusion and counting.** A matter is counted only if it concerns health care, alleges a claim on, or payment obtained from, a payer, is the subject of its own Justice Department release or filing, and dates from 2023 or later. Matters with no claim on a payer (for example unlawful prescribing for cash) are kept on a watch list and not counted. The rules, with their reasons, are in `docs/How_the_case_base_is_scoped.md`. The re-screening was done by AI agents on one model, checked internally and by a second AI reader on a sample; it is not independent review. The categories, lifecycle stages and control components assigned to counted rows were not re-coded in this release. A re-read of 50 randomly drawn counted rows against the official text found no row that failed the inclusion tests, but 9 of 50 (18%) had a category that clearly does not fit its definition and 8 more were arguable; stage and component were not tested and are likely to carry at least the same error. Per-category counts and dollar totals should be read as approximate.
- **What the controls found.** Internal tests of the evidence found that many controls are general rather than tied to a scheme, that several are run by the party under suspicion, and that the case base says little about corrective action. A count of cases behind a control is evidence of a detected, prosecuted pattern, not of how common it is.
- **Detection tests.** The 128 detection tests are reference patterns written for adaptation, run only on invented data. A first run on 6 October 2026 found forty with a known defect; they were fixed and the pack run again on 10 November 2026; a further review by AI readers then found more logic errors in situations the invented data does not exercise, and those tests were fixed again. On the final run on 11 November 2026, 127 of the 128 flagged every planted case and none of the named look-alikes on the invented dataset, and one cannot be tested on claims data alone. Known open points are listed in each test's notes. The toolkit shows each result beside its test. A clean run on invented data says nothing about how a query behaves on a real warehouse. They produce candidates for review, never a denial or payment action. No detection test covers the financial-relationships (FR) controls of Module 09.
- **Hold-out test.** An internal hold-out test took 65 later cases, set aside 3 that were not health care payment fraud, and asked of the remaining 62 whether the library holds a control that addresses the main failure in the case. It found one in 33 of 62 (53 percent), below the 60 percent line set beforehand, so the result counts against the library. A stricter second reading, made after the fact, gives 15 of 62 (24 percent). Leaving out the Module 09 controls, which were written after those cases began to be collected, the first figure is 31 of 62 (50 percent). The cases were read and rated by AI readers on one model, and the two halves were rated by different readers; this is not independent review.
- **Control gaps the tests found.** The tests found no control in the library dedicated to four things: confirming with the member that billed services were received; checking a claimed acquisition cost against the invoice; the validity of a laboratory test at the point of payment; and a treating relationship between the ordering clinician and the patient at claim time. This release does not add them. The library is written from the payer and provider side; oversight by regulators and licensing boards (for example of controlled-substance prescribing) is out of scope.
- **Answer order.** In 165 of the 247 questions the best-scoring answer is the first option listed, so a respondent can score well by picking the first option without reading. This is a known limitation of the instrument; the options were not re-ordered in this release.
- **Content without subject-matter review.** Control MC-29 and its assessment question were added from public rule text and have not had subject-matter review of how the answer options are scored. The question is in the Deep tier, so it does not change the Core score. The same caveat covers Module 09 (Financial Relationships & Referral Integrity): its controls and its 22 questions were written by hand from public sources, and neither its regulatory anchors nor the scoring of its answer options has had subject-matter review.

## Appropriate use

Use HPI-CT to organize an internal gap analysis, prioritize where to invest control effort, prepare for (not substitute for) an audit or an OIG-based program review, and structure conversations with compliance, internal audit, and leadership. Treat every output as a **prompt for professional judgment**, not a conclusion.
